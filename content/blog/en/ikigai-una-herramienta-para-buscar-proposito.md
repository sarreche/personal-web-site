Some questions feel so important that they can leave us completely still.

“What is my purpose?” is one of them.

It sounds as though an exact answer exists somewhere, waiting to be discovered. One profession, mission, or sentence capable of organizing the rest of our life. Until it appears, we feel as though we are improvising.

The problem is that waiting for a definitive revelation can become another way of not moving.

That is why I am interested in **ikigai**. Not as a Japanese formula that promises to solve life in one afternoon, but as a tool for asking better questions and beginning to test small answers.

## What ikigai means

*Ikigai* is a Japanese word often translated as “reason for living,” “that which makes life worth living,” or “what gives us a reason to get up.” No single translation captures the entire concept.

In Japan, it can refer to something large, but also to everyday sources of value and joy: caring for someone, cultivating a garden, participating in a community, practicing an activity, or doing work that feels meaningful. It does not have to be a career, a company, or a world-changing mission.

The [Government of Japan's introduction to the concept](https://www.japan.go.jp/kizuna/2022/03/ikigai_japanese_secret_to_a_joyful_life.html) describes it broadly: that which brings value and joy to life, from loved ones to work and hobbies.

That already changes the question considerably. Perhaps purpose is not one enormous answer. Perhaps it also lives in specific activities and relationships that make a day feel worthwhile.

## The famous diagram is not all of ikigai

You have probably seen the four-circle diagram:

- What you love.
- What you are good at.
- What the world needs.
- What you can be paid for.

The word *ikigai* appears at the center of all four.

The diagram is useful, but it helps to know what we are using. It is not a traditional Japanese representation of the concept. It is a Western adaptation that combined an earlier purpose diagram with the word *ikigai* and became popular through books, workshops, and social media.

Academic literature itself notes that there is no agreed definition of ikigai and that the four-circle model is not equivalent to its Japanese cultural meaning. That does not mean we need to discard it.

We can use it as a **map for personal and professional exploration**, rather than a scientific test or a complete definition of a tradition.

The mistake would be believing that our life is poorly designed if we cannot find one activity exactly at the center.

## First question: what genuinely matters to me?

The “what you love” circle too quickly becomes “what is my great passion?” That word can create more pressure than clarity.

I would begin with smaller questions:

- Which activities make me lose track of time?
- Which subjects do I keep reading about when nobody asks me to?
- Which problems bother me enough that I try to improve them?
- What kind of conversation leaves me with more energy?
- Which part of a difficult week would I still want to keep?

We are not looking for a perfect activity. We are looking for signals.

I would also distinguish between what I enjoy consuming and what I enjoy practicing. I can love listening to music without wanting to spend years learning an instrument. I can admire science without enjoying the daily work of research.

Purpose has to coexist with the reality of doing, not only the fantasy of being.

## Second question: what can I do, and what am I willing to learn?

“What you are good at” can sound like an assessment of finished talents. Our capabilities are not a closed list, though.

It helps to consider at least three things:

1. What we already do with relative ease.
2. What other people regularly ask us to do.
3. What we do not yet master but would willingly practice for a long time.

We sometimes underestimate abilities because they feel natural. Listening, explaining an idea, noticing inconsistencies, organizing a project, or remaining calm around a problem may feel like details. To somebody else, they may be exactly the value they need.

It is also important not to confuse being good with never making mistakes. Many valuable capabilities appear after enough frustration to recognize patterns and make better decisions.

The question is not only “What am I talented at?” It is also “Which effort is worth investing in?”

## Third question: whom can I help, and with which problem?

“What the world needs” is an enormous phrase. If we try to answer for all humanity, we will probably write something as correct as it is useless: peace, education, health, equality.

We can bring the question closer:

- Which people do I understand well?
- Which difficulty do they encounter repeatedly?
- Which task consumes their time, energy, or peace of mind?
- Which small change would genuinely improve their day?
- Which problem can I understand from close range?

Not every purpose has to solve a global crisis. Designing a clearer tool, teaching patiently, caring for somebody, or creating a space where others feel supported can also create meaning.

For this conversation, the world can begin with one concrete community.

## Fourth question: how can it be sustained?

The money circle often creates discomfort. It seems to contaminate a conversation that should be more elevated.

But an activity also has to coexist with time, housing, health, and responsibilities. Asking how it can be sustained does not make it less noble. It makes it possible.

This does not mean all our ikigai must become employment. A deep source of meaning can exist outside the market. In fact, forcing a beloved activity to pay every bill can change our relationship with it.

We can separate two questions:

- Which work can finance the life I want?
- Which activities make that life valuable to me?

Sometimes they coincide. Sometimes they partially overlap. Sometimes they need to remain separate.

The diagram is more useful when it reveals those tensions than when it forces us to pretend there is a perfect intersection.

## Do not look for an answer; design experiments

Completing the four circles can produce a page full of beautiful words and no decision.

To prevent that, I would turn promising intersections into small experiments.

If you are interested in teaching and other people value your explanations, prepare a short lesson or mentor somebody for two weeks. If you are drawn to solving problems for small businesses, talk to five owners before designing a company. If you think you want to write, publish four pieces before imagining a new professional identity.

A good experiment should answer something you do not yet know:

- Do I enjoy the practice or only the idea?
- Can I sustain it once it stops feeling new?
- Does it genuinely help another person?
- Do I want to improve at this for years?
- Is there a reasonable way to make space for it in my life?

Purpose does not always appear through more thought. It often becomes visible after we act and pay attention to what happens.

## Ikigai can change

Another trap is believing that we need to find one answer forever.

People change. Our abilities, relationships, obligations, and opportunities change. Something that created meaning at twenty may not organize life at forty. A period of care, learning, or recovery can completely reshape our priorities.

That does not mean the earlier purpose was false. It means it served a function at a particular time.

We can revisit the exercise periodically:

- What remains alive in my previous answers?
- What am I doing only from inertia?
- Which new capability has appeared?
- Which need in other people can I now see?
- Which cost am I no longer willing to pay?

Ikigai can be an orientation that evolves, not a lifelong contract.

## Having purpose does not mean feeling motivated every day

Finding direction does not eliminate fatigue, doubt, or boring tasks.

Meaningful work still includes emails, administrative problems, and days when nothing works. An activity we love can still require discipline. Helping others can exhaust us when there are no boundaries.

Purpose does not turn every moment into happiness. It offers a reason for moving through parts that would not be enjoyable on their own.

We should also be careful with health promises. Longitudinal studies in Japan have found associations between having ikigai and better wellbeing among older adults. For example, a [study published in 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8814687/) observed associations with lower risks of functional disability and dementia, as well as higher life satisfaction.

These are associations in specific populations and contexts. They do not prove that completing a diagram causes a longer life, nor that a lack of purpose makes somebody responsible for illness or hardship.

Ikigai can help us think. It is not a medical prescription or a guarantee.

## An exercise to begin this week

If you want to try the tool without turning it into another endless self-discovery task, you can do this:

1. Set aside twenty minutes and write quick answers for the four circles. Do not edit too much.
2. Mark three elements that seem related.
3. Choose one combination that makes you curious, not necessarily the grandest one.
4. Design one action you can complete within seven days.
5. After doing it, note what gave you energy, what drained you, whom it helped, and what you learned.

The final step matters most. The exercise does not end on paper. It ends when experience changes the map.

## Purpose may be more ordinary than we imagine

I like ikigai because it puts several important dimensions on the table. I find it even more valuable when it stops being a search for perfection.

We do not need to discover a profession that we love every minute, perform better than anybody else, use to solve a global need, and receive extraordinary pay for. That intersection may exist, but making it a requirement is an excellent way to feel that we never arrive.

We can begin with something more human: a direction that carries enough meaning, uses capabilities we want to develop, contributes something to other people, and has a sustainable place in our life.

Perhaps purpose is not a secret hidden inside us. Perhaps it is a relationship we build between what we value, what we practice, and the way we participate in the world.

That relationship is not discovered once.

It is tested, cared for, and chosen again.
