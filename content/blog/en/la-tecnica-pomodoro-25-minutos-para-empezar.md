Some days the task itself doesn't look that hard, but sitting down to start it does. You open the document, check a message, return to the document, remember something else. By the time you find your way in, you're already thinking about everything still left to do.

I like the Pomodoro Technique for a fairly modest reason: it doesn't ask you to solve the entire day. It asks you to choose one task and give it a stretch of genuine attention.

It's an old idea. Maybe that's exactly why it's worth revisiting at a time when we have tools to do almost anything faster, but not necessarily an easier time focusing.

## The method in a nutshell

Francesco Cirillo created the [Pomodoro Technique](https://www.pomodorotechnique.com/) when he was a student. He started with a tomato-shaped kitchen timer — *pomodoro* is Italian for tomato. The best-known version suggests working on one task for **25 minutes**, then taking a **short break of about 5 minutes**. After several rounds, take a longer break.

You can try it today without installing anything:

1. Choose a specific task. “Make progress on the project” is too broad; “write the first paragraph” or “review this function” gives you a place to begin.
2. Set a timer for 25 minutes and put notifications aside if you can.
3. Stay with that task until the timer rings. If another idea or to-do comes up, jot it down for later unless it's truly urgent.
4. Take a short break. Stand up, get some water, look out the window. The break is part of the plan too.
5. Choose what comes next. You can continue, reshape the task, or admit you need help before going further.

You don't need to complete a heroic number of rounds. One can be enough to get an otherwise stuck morning moving.

## The valuable part isn't just the clock

Twenty-five minutes is easy to remember, but it isn't a magic number. What changes the experience is giving work a boundary. Faced with something that could swallow the whole afternoon, saying “I'll stay with this until the timer rings” feels much less intimidating.

It also makes interruptions visible. Some come from other people; others come from us. The sudden urge to check email right when a sentence gets difficult. The temptation to reorganize folders before writing. The point isn't to punish yourself for getting distracted. It's to notice what pulls you away and decide whether it can wait.

The [full method](https://www.pomodorotechnique.com/pomodoro-self-paced/) goes beyond starting a timer: it also involves planning, tracking interruptions, and learning to estimate effort. You don't have to adopt all of that at once. But it's worth remembering that Pomodoro isn't a contest to pile up blocks of time. It's a way to understand how you work.

## When 25 minutes doesn't fit

If you're in an important conversation, handling an emergency, or deeply immersed in an idea, you don't have to stop because an alarm says so. And if a task takes longer to get into, try longer blocks. The timer should help you, not become another boss.

Not every day gives you a protected half hour, either. Sometimes you need to be available to others, care for someone, or respond to work that changes by the minute. In those cases, a shorter, realistic stretch — or simply a genuine break — may be more useful.

I wouldn't measure a day's worth by the number of pomodoros completed. You can fill a page with check marks without moving the important work forward. Or you can do just one and finally have that conversation, write that draft, or understand the problem you've been avoiding.

## A gentler way to come back

Maybe the best part of this technique isn't a promise of productivity, but permission to start again. If you get distracted, you haven't ruined the day. You can notice what happened, choose the task again, and give it another 25 minutes. If you're tired, you can actually rest.
